\title{Tools, Not Talk:\\The Knowledge Ceiling of Biology Agents}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{abstract}
Agentic systems for biology are increasingly built around orchestration: critics that review answers, teams of role-playing scientists that debate, and models that reason for longer.
We show that on objectively scored biology research tasks this machinery adds little beyond what matched independent sampling provides, and we identify why.
Across 150 pre-registered LAB-Bench and LABBench2 questions, compared at a matched budget of three model calls, self-critique never beats simple majority voting, multi-agent debate helps only a model whose reasoning is switched off, and a \TokRatioOSS-fold increase in generated tokens from longer reasoning does not improve accuracy.
The reason is a ceiling set by the questions themselves: which question is asked explains \ItemShareMain\% of the variance in correctness, while the choice of model, strategy and reasoning effort explains \ArmShareMain\%.
That ceiling is largely missing knowledge rather than missing deliberation.
A single model call with web search and code execution solves \ToolsYesUnsolved\% of the questions that all eight orchestrated systems in our main study failed.
For life-science agents, the budget belongs in tools, not talk.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
LLM agents now plan experiments, analyse data and generate hypotheses in chemistry and biology \citep{boiko2023coscientist,bran2023chemcrow,swanson2024virtuallab,huang2025biomni,gottweis2026coscientist,ghareeb2026robin}.
Many of these systems are organised as conversations: a critic reviews a draft, several specialised scientists debate, an adjudicator decides.
Their gains are usually reported against a single model call, even though the agent makes many.
Sampling several answers and voting already improves accuracy \citep{wang2022selfconsistency,li2024moreagents,brown2024monkeys}, so these comparisons cannot tell whether the orchestration helps or whether the extra calls do \citep{kapoor2024agentsmatters}.

We separate the two.
Every orchestrated strategy receives exactly the budget of a voting baseline: three calls, the same model, the same prompts and the same decoding.
We then ask what actually moves accuracy on biology research tasks: orchestration, longer reasoning, or access to knowledge through tools.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{Orchestration adds little at matched compute.} Across three models, self-critique never beats voting and breaks at least as many answers as it repairs. Debate helps only when the model cannot reason on its own. Raising reasoning effort, for six model families, rarely helps either (\S\ref{sec:orch}--\ref{sec:think}).
\item \textbf{The question decides.} Systems largely solve the same questions and fail the same questions. Question identity explains \ItemShareMain\% of the variance in correctness and the system \ArmShareMain\% (\S\ref{sec:ceiling}).
\item \textbf{Tools break the ceiling.} One tool-using call solves \ToolsYesUnsolved\% of the questions that no main-study system solved, and more compute on top of tools adds comparatively little (\S\ref{sec:tools}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Setup}
\paragraph{Tasks.}
We draw 30 questions from each of five categories (150 in total): four from LAB-Bench \citep{laurent2024labbench} (protocol troubleshooting, sequence manipulation, database and literature questions; multiple choice) and open-ended sequence computations from LABBench2 SeqQA2 \citep{labbench2}, scored with the official validators.
All answers are scored deterministically, with no LLM judge.

\paragraph{Strategies.}
\direct{} makes one call.
\vote{} makes three independent calls and takes the majority answer.
\critique{} solves, critiques its own solution, then revises.
\debate{} has a molecular biologist and a skeptical reviewer solve independently, then an adjudicator decides.
\vote{}, \critique{} and \debate{} each use exactly three calls, and only visible outputs pass between calls (prompts in App.~\ref{app:prompts}).

\paragraph{Models.}
The matched-budget study uses GPT-OSS-120B \citep{openai2025gptoss} (low reasoning effort), Gemma~4~31B \citep{google2026gemma4} (thinking disabled) and Gemini~3.5 Flash-Lite (low thinking) on all 150 questions (Flash-Lite on a fixed 75).
To test reasoning effort and tools, we add GPT-6 Luna, GPT-6 Sol and GPT-5.6 Terra, and rerun GPT-OSS and Flash-Lite at high effort, on a fixed 30-question subset with 6 per category.
Task IDs, prompts, decoding and the primary analysis were frozen before evaluation (App.~\ref{app:deviations}). All contrasts are paired over questions, with bootstrap 95\% CIs and exact McNemar tests.

\subsection{Orchestration adds little at matched compute}\label{sec:orch}
Three calls spent on orchestration generally do no better than three calls spent on independent samples (Table~\ref{tab:main}).
\textbf{Self-critique does not beat voting} for any of the three models ($\DCritiqueMinusVoteOSS$, $\DCritiqueMinusVoteGemma$, $\DCritiqueMinusVoteLite$ points; pooled $\DPooledCritiqueMinusVote$ [$\DPooledCritiqueMinusVoteLo$, $\DPooledCritiqueMinusVoteHi$]).
It breaks correct answers as readily as it fixes wrong ones: relative to the vote it rescues \RescueCritiqueOSS\%, \RescueCritiqueGemma\% and \RescueCritiqueLite\% of errors but corrupts \CorruptCritiqueOSS\%, \CorruptCritiqueGemma\% and \CorruptCritiqueLite\% of correct answers.
The damage is worst on exact sequence manipulation (SeqQA: \CritMinusVoteSeqQAOSS{} and \CritMinusVoteSeqQALite{} points for GPT-OSS and Flash-Lite), where a fluent but wrong critique argues the model out of a correct computation.
\textbf{Debate helps only the model that cannot think.} For Gemma, whose thinking is disabled, debate beats voting by \DDebateMinusVoteGemma{} points ($p=\PDebateMinusVoteGemma$); for the two models that reason, it does not ($\DDebateMinusVoteOSS$ and $\DDebateMinusVoteLite$).

\subsection{Longer reasoning does not substitute for knowledge}\label{sec:think}
For most models, raising reasoning effort multiplies the cost without moving accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:tvt}).
GPT-OSS at high effort generates \TokRatioOSS$\times$ more tokens and scores \TDirectHighOSS\% against \TDirectLowOSS\%; Flash-Lite spends \TokRatioLite$\times$ more tokens and scores \TDirectHighLite\% against \TDirectLowLite\%.
The one model that gains is GPT-6 Luna, which uses no reasoning tokens at low effort, and its high-effort single call reaches exactly the accuracy its low-effort debate already had (\TDirectHighLuna\%).
Debate and reasoning are therefore substitutes, and neither gets past the plateau.
Stacking orchestration on high effort adds little: GPT-6 Sol scores \TDirectHighSol\% with every strategy while the three-call strategies generate ${\sim}3\times$ the tokens.

\subsection{The question, not the system, decides}\label{sec:ceiling}
A two-way decomposition of the correctness matrix attributes \ItemShareMain\% of the variance to the question and \ArmShareMain\% to the system (Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling}).
The pattern holds across all \NArmsThirty{} model, strategy and effort configurations on the shared subset, frontier models at high effort included (\ItemShareThirty\% vs.\ \ArmShareThirty\%).
Systems largely solve the same easy questions and fail the same hard ones.
The \NUnsolved{} questions that no main-study system solves are dominated by literature and database recall (\NUnsolvedRecall{}) and open-ended sequence computation (\NUnsolvedSeqQAII{}).
These are failures of knowledge, which additional deliberation did not supply in any configuration we tested.

\subsection{Tools break the ceiling}\label{sec:tools}
Giving GPT-6 Luna web search and code execution in a single call raises accuracy on the shared subset from \ToolsNoThirty\% to \ToolsYesThirty\% (\ToolsDThirty{} points [\ToolsDThirtyLo, \ToolsDThirtyHi], $p=\ToolsPThirty$).
That is more than any orchestration or reasoning setting achieves, at ${\sim}$\ToolsTokThirty{} generated tokens per question.
On the \ToolsNUnsolved{} questions that all eight main-study systems (two models $\times$ four strategies) failed, the same single call reaches \ToolsYesUnsolved\%, against \ToolsNoUnsolved\% without tools (\ToolsDUnsolved{} points [\ToolsDUnsolvedLo, \ToolsDUnsolvedHi], $p=\ToolsPUnsolved$).
Literature questions rise from \ToolsLitNoUnsolved\% to \ToolsLitYesUnsolved\% and database questions from \ToolsDbNoUnsolved\% to \ToolsDbYesUnsolved\%.
Retrieval helping on these categories is expected, since they were designed for retrieval agents \citep{laurent2024labbench,futurehouse2024paperqa2}. The point is the contrast: none of the eight orchestrated systems solved any of them.
Compute on top of tools adds comparatively little. High effort reaches \ToolsHighAccThirty\% ($\ToolsHighDThirty$, $p=\ToolsHighPThirty$) and a three-call vote \ToolsVoteAccThirty\% ($\ToolsVoteDThirty$, $p=\ToolsVotePThirty$), at ${\sim}6\times$ and ${\sim}3.5\times$ the tokens.
Tool calls never reached the benchmark or its datasets.

\section{Implications for building life-science agents}
\textbf{Benchmark against a matched vote.} A multi-call agent should beat independent sampling with the same number of calls, not a single call \citep{kapoor2024agentsmatters}. Most orchestration gains disappear under this baseline, consistent with compute-matched studies of debate in general reasoning \citep{wang2024rethinking,smit2024mad,choi2025debatevsvote,wu2025candebate} and with the unreliability of intrinsic self-correction \citep{huang2023cannotcorrect}.
\textbf{Spend the budget on tools, not talk.} Critics, debaters and longer chains of thought re-deliberate over knowledge the model does not have. Retrieval supplies it, at a fraction of the tokens.
\textbf{Build domain tools for computation.} A generic shell barely helps open-ended sequence design and analysis (\ToolsSeqQAIINoUnsolved\% to \ToolsSeqQAIIYesUnsolved\%). The next gains lie in biology-specific tool ecosystems and specialist models, not in more conversation between copies of the same model.

\paragraph{Scope.}
Our tasks have deterministic answers, and we study short-horizon question answering rather than long-running discovery loops. The frontier and tool comparisons use a 30-question subset, and the tool condition adds a one-sentence note announcing the tools.

\section{Conclusion}
On biology research tasks, how a model spends extra inference barely changes which questions it gets right. This holds for voting, critique, debate and longer reasoning alike: the question explains \ItemShareMain\% of the variance in correctness and the system \ArmShareMain\%.
The ceiling is largely knowledge, and a single tool-using call lifts it where orchestration did not.
Life-science agents should spend their budget on tools, not talk.

\section{Pre-registration deviations}\label{app:deviations}
All task IDs, prompts, decoding settings and the primary analysis were frozen after a 10-question pilot and before any main-set call. Changes made after the freeze:
(i)~throttle and retry fixes for an undocumented 150 requests/hour provider limit (no completed call was re-run);
(ii)~22 GPT-OSS questions served in full by a second provider of the same model (identical prompts, seeds and cap; excluding them leaves \debate{} $-$ \vote{} at $\DDebateMinusVoteOSSNoGroq$), with partially served questions discarded and re-run on the primary provider;
(iii)~the reasoning-effort and tool arms on the fixed 30-question subset and the unsolved set, added after the freeze. Frontier models were served through their vendor CLI with web search, code execution and sub-agents disabled except in the tool condition (a first attempt with web search left on was discarded unanalysed); GPT-OSS at high effort was served by a second host whose low-effort accuracy matches the main run. The tool condition prepends one sentence announcing the tools, and all searches and commands were logged and checked for benchmark access (none).
